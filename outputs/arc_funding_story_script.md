# ARC Grants 2010–2026: Speaker Script

Companion script for [The Story in 15 Slides](../site/arc_funding_story.html). Roughly 1 minute per slide, about 16 minutes in total. Stage cues are in *[brackets]*.

---

## 1. Fewer, bigger, more concentrated

*[Let the title sit for a beat before you speak.]*

Here's a question every researcher in this room has asked at 2 a.m. while writing a grant: *where is the money actually going?*

Not where we think it's going, or where the rumours say it's going, but where it actually lands. So we went and looked. Every funded ARC grant from 2010 to 2026. More than 20,000 of them.

The title gives away the punchline: **fewer, bigger, more concentrated.** But the interesting part is the detail underneath it. Which fields are winning, which universities are quietly climbing, and which ones are quietly sliding.

We'll go from the big picture, to fields, to universities, to individual people. Then I'll tell you what *not* to conclude from any of it.

---

## 2. Every funded grant, 2010 to 2026

Three schemes make up our dataset.

**Eleven thousand Discovery Projects**, the ARC's flagship investigator-led grants. **Five thousand fellowships**, from early-career DECRAs through to Laureates. And **nearly four thousand Linkage Projects**, where researchers team up with industry or government.

Fifty-nine institutions in total.

*[Point to the orange box.]*

Before we go further, one honest warning. This is a list of **winners**. It's the trophy cabinet, not the entry list. We can tell you who took home grants. We can't tell you how many people applied and missed out. So whenever you're tempted to say "it's got harder to win", hold that thought. This data can't answer that question.

---

## 3. Fewer grants, each worth far more

This is the slide that explains everything else.

*[Left chart.]* In 2010 the ARC funded **925** Discovery Projects. In 2026 it funded **526**. That's down 43%, and in 2024 it bottomed out at just 421.

*[Right chart.]* Now look at the size of each grant. The median went from **$310,000 to $676,000**, which is more than double. Most of that jump happened after 2023.

So is the ARC spending less? No. The total pot sat at roughly a quarter of a billion dollars a year for over a decade, then rose to $376 million in 2026.

Same cake, fewer slices, much bigger slices.

A quick caveat: these are nominal dollars, so some of that rise is inflation. But inflation didn't double anything between 2010 and 2026. This is a real change in strategy.

---

## 4. Grants now go to bigger teams

If the slices are bigger, who's eating them?

In 2010, almost **half** of Discovery Projects had a single chief investigator: one person, one idea, one grant. Today it's **one in five**.

The average team grew from under two chief investigators to more than two and a half.

*[Point to the annotation.]* Look at that cliff between 2022 and 2023. It's a gradual slide for twelve years, then a sudden drop. Something happened there. Perhaps a rule change, perhaps a change in how applications were assessed. The data doesn't tell us, and I'd want to check before blaming anyone.

But the practical message is clear: **the lone-genius grant is becoming an endangered species.**

---

## 5. Biology and engineering take a bigger slice

Now let's ask which *fields* are getting those bigger slices.

*[Blue bars.]* Two clear winners: **biological sciences**, up five points, and **engineering**, up four. Together they now appear on almost half of all Discovery Projects.

*[Orange bars.]* And the losses? They're spread widely: maths, earth sciences, history, the social sciences. Look at **economics**, which has gone from 5.3% to 2.7%. Its share has halved.

Now, the skeptic in the room is thinking: "Didn't the field codes change in 2023? Isn't this just a relabelling?" Good instinct. But these trends were already under way *before* the reclassification. This is a real shift, not an accounting quirk.

---

## 6. AI goes from rare to routine

Fields are broad buckets. What about *topics*? We searched every grant summary for mentions of AI and machine learning.

*[Trace the line with your hand.]* For a decade it's flat, under 1%, basically a rounding error. Then 2020 arrives and the line takes off. By 2025, **one in twenty** Discovery Projects mentions AI. In 2010 it was about one in five hundred.

*[Point to the COVID box.]* And here's a nice contrast. COVID-19 appeared in grant summaries in 2022, peaked at about 1.5%, and is already fading. That's what a topic driven by an event looks like. AI looks like a lasting trend, and it isn't fading.

This is a simple keyword search, so treat the exact numbers loosely. The shape of the curve is the point.

---

## 7. The Go8 wins two in three grants, every year

Now, universities. And the obvious first question: do the big eight run the show?

Yes. The Group of Eight wins about **two out of every three** Discovery Projects, and about two in three fellowships.

But what really stands out is how *flat* these lines are. 68% at the start, 66% at the end. Sixteen years of funding rounds, policy changes and a pandemic, and the line barely moves.

If you only looked at this slide, you'd conclude nothing has changed.

*[Pause.]*

You'd be wrong.

---

## 8. Underneath, the Go8's strengths are shifting

That flat line hides a quiet reshuffle.

*[Top half.]* The Go8 is **tightening its grip** on biomedical and clinical sciences, agriculture, maths and biology, up seven to eight points in several of them. These are the expensive, lab-heavy fields, plus theory-heavy maths, where scale matters.

*[Bottom half.]* But it's **losing ground** in engineering, chemistry, physics, earth sciences, and most of all in the humanities. In history and in language and culture, the Go8 share has fallen by about 11 points each. In language and culture, it's now *below half*.

So the Go8's overall share hasn't moved, but its gains in some fields are cancelling out losses in others.

*[Footer.]* And if you're wondering about law, it fell the most of all, from 75% to 49%. But that's only around ten grants a year, so I wouldn't read much into it yet.

---

## 9. Victoria has overtaken New South Wales

Now let's look at states, because there's been a change at the top.

For the early 2010s, New South Wales was the clear number one at a third of all Discovery Projects. Victoria trailed at 26%.

Today? **Victoria: 31.6%. New South Wales: 30.7%.** Victoria has moved into first place.

Most of that gain is one university, which you'll meet on the next slide, with La Trobe, RMIT and Deakin also growing.

Elsewhere, the ACT's drop is almost entirely the ANU. Western Australia has slipped too. And South Australia has held steady the whole time.

---

## 10. Monash rises, ANU slips, tech universities climb

Here's the one university I mentioned: **Monash.**

Monash went from 9.5% of all Discovery Projects to **12.6%**. It's now the single biggest winner in the country, driven by biology, biomedicine and economics.

At the other end is the **ANU**, down from 10% to just over 7%. It used to dominate physics and earth sciences, and those are exactly the fields where it has lost the most.

*[Middle of the chart.]* The quieter story is the **technology universities**. QUT, RMIT and UTS each gained a point or more, mostly in engineering, chemistry and environmental science. And Flinders has doubled its share.

*[Footer.]* One more number to note: in biomedical and clinical sciences, the top three universities now win **two thirds** of all projects. Ten years ago it was under half. So concentration isn't only rising at the national level. Within individual fields, it's rising even faster.

---

## 11. Citations don't explain who wins

Here's a question people always ask: are the Go8 winning because they have the most-cited researchers?

We checked. For every Discovery Project since 2016, we took the lead investigator's citations in the three years before they applied. Then we compared each of them only with other winners in the same field, in the same year. Historians against historians, biologists against biologists.

*[Right column.]* The Go8 answer is no. Their lead CIs sit right at the middle, **50/50**, every period. The Go8 win two in three grants, but not because their leads out-cite everyone else's.

*[Dot plot.]* And look at who *is* above the line. QUT, Curtin and RMIT lead CIs are more cited than about **60%** of their peers. Monash, the biggest winner of all, sits slightly *below* the middle. Monash wins on volume. The technology universities are winning with fewer, but very well-cited, people.

*[Orange box.]* Now, one way to read this is a **halo effect**. Go8 researchers are generally more cited, so you'd expect Go8 *winners* to be more cited too. They aren't. That would fit a world where a Go8 address carries a less-cited lead over the line, while someone at QUT needs a stronger record to win. And it's not that the Go8 lets more juniors lead: the career ages are identical. But we only see the winners, not the applicants, so this is a hypothesis, not a finding.

Two more things. A stronger citation record buys you only a slightly bigger grant. And the bar hasn't risen: lead CIs' citations have doubled since 2016, but so has the whole of science's.

So if citations don't explain who wins, something else does: field, institution, team, the proposal itself. And this data can't see any of those.

---

## 12. Linkage Projects are a shrinking share

Let's talk about industry.

Linkage Projects are the ARC's main bridge between universities and the outside world. In the early 2010s they made up 30% of the two main project schemes. Now they're **24%**.

*[Point to the box.]* There was a real dip in 2017 to 2019, when Linkage awards fell from 235 a year to between 87 and 137.

*[Right chart.]* And look at how differently universities behave. RMIT and UTS get **more than a third** of their project grants through Linkage. ANU and Monash get about **one in nine**.

Put that next to the last slide and you can see a pattern: **the universities that win most in Discovery tend to be the least industry-facing, and the reverse holds too.**

One to watch: Adelaide sits near the bottom here at 12%, but it has just merged with UniSA, which was the *most* Linkage-oriented university in the country a decade ago. That combination could look very different in a few years.

---

## 13. Early-career fellows: who keeps them?

Now let's follow people instead of institutions.

Using ORCID IDs, we tracked **628 early-career DECRA fellows** who started between 2012 and 2016.

*[Move left to right across the boxes.]*

About three in ten of them went on to win a mid-career Future Fellowship, typically five and a half years later.

And of those, **three in ten won it at a different university.**

*[Bottom row.]* So where did they go? The net gainers are Deakin, RMIT, Macquarie and QUT. The net losers are UNSW, Monash and Queensland.

Put simply, **the big universities train the early-career researchers, and the growing universities hire them** once they've proven themselves.

Now, the fine print. It's only sixty moves in total, and ORCID coverage from those years is patchy. So treat this as a lead worth following up, not a firm finding.

---

## 14. Six things to keep in mind

*[Slower pace here. This slide is what keeps your audience from misquoting you.]*

Before anyone tweets any of this, here are six caveats.

**One:** this data only includes winners. It tells us nothing about success rates.

**Two:** the field codes changed in 2023. Be suspicious of any jump that happens exactly at that line.

**Three:** a grant with three fields counts three times, so field shares add up to more than 100%.

**Four:** universities are named as the ARC records them. Adelaide's merger already shows up from 2024.

**Five:** tracking people depends on ORCID, and plenty of early records don't have one.

**Six:** 2026 isn't finished. Only the DECRA round is in so far.

None of these overturn the story. They just tell you how far each number can be trusted.

---

## 15. Four things that changed

*[Count them off on your fingers.]*

**One.** The ARC funds fewer, bigger grants for bigger teams. Awards are down 43%, the median grant is up 118%, and solo projects have fallen from almost half to one in five.

**Two.** Life sciences and engineering are growing, while maths, earth sciences, economics and the humanities are shrinking.

**Three.** The Go8 still wins two in three, but underneath that, Monash is pulling ahead, the ANU is slipping, and the technology universities are rising.

**Four.** Victoria is now the biggest state for Discovery Projects.

*[Point to the URL.]*

Everything you've seen today is on the ARC Research Hub. You can filter it by field, by scheme, by state and by university. Go and find your own institution, because I'd bet there's a story in there that none of us have spotted yet.

Thank you.
