Notes

This deck walks from the big picture (how much money, how many grants) down to fields of research, then to universities: Go8 versus the rest, states, and individual institutions. It closes with the early-career pipeline and the caveats you need to read the numbers fairly. Everything here is based on funded grants listed in the ARC Data Portal with funding commencing 2010 to 2026.

Notes

Three schemes. Discovery Projects are the ARC’s main investigator-led grants. Fellowships fund individual researchers at three career stages: DECRA for early career, Future Fellowships mid-career, Laureate Fellowships for senior leaders. Linkage Projects pair university researchers with industry or other partners. Discovery Projects and fellowships carry field of research codes; Linkage Projects in this dataset do not, so they appear only in the funding and industry-mix slides. Important: this is a list of winners. Without application numbers we cannot talk about success rates.

Notes

The headline story. The number of Discovery Projects awarded each year has fallen from 925 in 2010 to 526 in 2026, with a low of 421 in 2024. Over the same period the median grant more than doubled, from about $310,000 to $676,000, with most of the rise after 2023. The total pot did not shrink: it sat between roughly $220 and $285 million a year from 2012 to 2024 and jumped to $342 million in 2025 and $376 million in 2026. So the money is being concentrated into fewer, larger projects. These are nominal dollars; part of the median rise is inflation. Because totals move this much, the rest of the deck compares shares rather than raw counts.

Notes

Alongside fewer, bigger grants, teams have grown. In 2010 almost half of Discovery Projects (46%) had a single chief investigator; by 2023 that had fallen to about one in five and it has stayed there. The average number of chief investigators per project rose from 1.86 to 2.60. The drop was gradual through the 2010s and then sharp between 2022 and 2023. The data does not tell us why; a rule or assessment change around that time is worth checking before attributing a cause. 285 early grants with no investigators recorded are excluded.

Notes

Which fields are winning a bigger share of Discovery Projects? Biological sciences and engineering have each grown by four to five percentage points and together now appear on almost half of all projects. The losers are spread across maths, earth sciences, economics and the humanities and social sciences. Economics has roughly halved its share. These shifts were already under way before the 2023 change in field codes, so they are not an artefact of reclassification. A grant that lists several fields counts in each, so shares do not add to 100%.

The Group of Eight research-intensive universities win about two thirds of ARC grants, and that share barely moves: 68% of Discovery Projects in 2010 to 2014, 66% in 2023 to 2026; 65% versus 63% for fellowships. Year-to-year wobbles are within a few points. So at the national level there is no dramatic shift away from or towards the Go8. Notes

The steady two-thirds hides a reshuffle. The Go8 is consolidating where research is expensive and lab-based or highly theoretical: biomedical and clinical sciences, agriculture, maths, biology, where its share has risen by four to eight points. Meanwhile other universities are winning a larger share in engineering, chemistry, physics and earth sciences, and especially in the humanities: history and language and culture are each down about 11 points for the Go8. Law dropped the most of all, from 75% to 49%, but on only about ten grants a year, so treat that as tentative.

By state, the big story is Victoria. Its share of Discovery Projects rose from 26% to almost 32%, overtaking New South Wales, which slipped from 33.5% to 30.7%. Most of Victoria’s gain is Monash, with La Trobe, RMIT and Deakin also growing. The ACT’s fall is almost entirely the ANU. Western Australia has also lost ground. South Australia has held steady at around 6 to 7%. Fellowships show the same pattern in milder form.

Notes

Individual universities. Monash is the standout: from 9.5% of all Discovery Projects to 12.6%, now the largest single winner, driven by biological, biomedical and economics research. The ANU has fallen from 10% to about 7%, with big drops in physics and earth sciences where it used to dominate. UWA has also lost ground. The technology universities, QUT, RMIT and UTS, have each grown their share by around one to one and a half points, mostly in engineering, chemistry and environmental science. Flinders doubled its share. Concentration is also rising in the life sciences: in biomedical and clinical sciences the top three universities now take two thirds of projects. These shifts were already visible before 2022.

Notes

Linkage Projects pair researchers with industry, government or community partners. As a share of the two main project schemes, Linkage has fallen from 30% to 24%, with a particularly sharp dip in 2017 to 2019. Universities differ a lot: the technology universities RMIT and UTS get over a third of their project grants through Linkage, while ANU, Monash and Adelaide get only 11 to 12%. In other words, the universities that dominate Discovery are the least industry-oriented, and vice versa. Adelaide’s low figure is worth watching now that it has merged with UniSA, which in 2010 to 2014 was the most Linkage-oriented university in the country.

Notes

Following individual researchers via their ORCID IDs. Of the early-career DECRA fellows who started between 2012 and 2016 and have an ORCID, about three in ten went on to win a Future Fellowship, typically five and a half years later. Of those, three in ten won it at a different university. The movers tend to flow from large Go8 universities to growing ones: Deakin, RMIT, Macquarie and QUT gained the most, UNSW, Monash and Queensland lost the most. In short, the big research universities train early-career researchers and others recruit them. These are small numbers, 60 moves in total, and ORCID coverage is incomplete for those years, so read this as a pattern worth investigating rather than a firm result.

Reading the numbers

Six things to keep in mind

Winners, not applicants

The data lists funded grants only, so it shows who won, not success rates.

Field codes changed in 2023

Older codes are mapped to the nearest new field. The mapping is approximate, so watch for steps at 2022–23.

Grants can count twice

A grant counts in every field it lists, so field shares add up to more than 100%.

Names as recorded

Each grant sits with its administering organisation. From 2024 some are listed under the merged Adelaide University.

People via ORCID

Tracking researchers relies on ORCID IDs, which are missing for many early records.

2026 is partial

Only the 2026 DECRA round is in the portal so far; other 2026 fellowships are still to come.

Dollar figures are nominal. Linkage Projects carry no field codes in this dataset.

Notes

Before drawing conclusions, a few cautions. Most importantly, this is a list of winners: without application numbers we cannot say whether grants became harder to win, only how the winners are distributed. The field-of-research classification changed in 2023, and older codes have been mapped to the new ones approximately, so be careful with any jump that happens exactly at 2022 to 2023. Grants with several fields count in each. Organisations are as recorded by the ARC, including the new Adelaide University name on some grants from 2024. Researcher-level analysis depends on ORCID IDs. And 2026 is incomplete for fellowships.

Takeaways

Four things that changed

1

Fewer, bigger grants for bigger teams. Awards down 43%, the median grant up 118%, solo projects down from 46% to 20%.

2

Life sciences and engineering are growing; maths, earth sciences, economics and the humanities are shrinking.

3

The Go8 still wins two in three, but Monash is pulling ahead, the ANU is slipping and technology universities are rising.

4

Victoria has overtaken New South Wales as the largest winner of Discovery Projects.

Notes

To sum up. One: the ARC is funding fewer, larger projects run by bigger teams. Two: the balance of fields is shifting towards the life sciences and engineering. Three: the Go8’s overall share is stable, but within it Monash is rising and the ANU falling, and outside it the technology universities are gaining ground, especially in engineering and the physical sciences. Four: Victoria is now the largest state by Discovery Projects. The interactive pages on the ARC Research Hub let you filter all of this by field, scheme, state and university.
